# The Dream Economy: Who Gets Rich Off Your Escapism

*The Low-Spec Metaverse · Part Three*

> Fixing reality is a bad business: if you succeed, you destroy your market.

*Published on June 12, 2026*

---

On October 28, 2021, Mark Zuckerberg renamed Facebook to Meta. In a 68-minute keynote he painted a grand vision: we will live inside an immersive virtual world, meeting, socializing, playing, and shopping there. He called it the metaverse.

Two-plus years and more than $40 billion later, Meta's metaverse division, Reality Labs, has accumulated losses far beyond expectations. Quest headset monthly actives are disappointing. Most people tried it once and left it gathering dust in a cabinet. The metaverse—at least the version Zuckerberg described—looks increasingly like an expensive miscalculation.

Meanwhile, on the other side of the Pacific, the vertical short-drama market reached nearly 37.4 billion yuan in 2023. Producers can make a short drama for tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand yuan, and the cycle of spending, recouping, and reinvesting has compressed to a few days. Some hits returned dozens of times their investment. The barrier to entry is low to a degree that's almost unsettling: a few people, a few phones, one rented room.

In the same period, a $40 billion substitute-reality project is struggling to survive, while a substitute-reality product requiring almost no technical sophistication is growing wild. The comparison deserves a careful look.

## A Brief History of Human Dream-Making

Zoom out for a moment.

Humanity's history of "manufacturing substitute realities" is much longer than most people realize. If you loosen the definition a little—anything that lets a person temporarily leave their present circumstances and enter another space of experience—then the earliest large-scale substitute-reality industry was probably religion.

That may make some people uncomfortable, but functionally, religion did provide a substitute-reality experience: a framework of meaning different from daily life. You suffer in this world, but the next world will compensate. Your individual life ends, but your soul is eternal. You sit at the bottom of the social hierarchy, but in God's eyes all are equal. These narratives gave people a reason to endure a world full of suffering. Marx's endlessly quoted line—"religion is the opium of the people"—is not entirely a metaphor in this sense. His word was *Opium des Volkes*, and opium in Europe at the time was a legal painkiller. Marx's point: religion relieves pain but does not treat the cause.

The medieval church also had some interesting economic parallels with today's content industry. You paid for your spiritual comfort through tithes and indulgences. The church supplied narratives (stories about heaven, hell, salvation); you supplied money and obedience. It was a subscription model for substitute reality.

Then came printing. Gutenberg's movable type in the 1440s transformed how narratives were distributed over the following two centuries. Books went from a luxury of nobles and clergy to a relatively mass consumer good. By the 18th century, the novel matured as a literary form—Richardson's *Pamela*, Defoe's *Robinson Crusoe*—and readers could, for the first time, immerse themselves in the complete life of a fictional character for an extended stretch of time.

That shift mattered. Before the novel, most narratives were either collective (religious ritual, oral tradition) or brief (a story told in one evening). The novel created an unprecedented experience: one person, alone, immersed in another world for a long time. This was privatized substitute reality. Samuel Johnson worried about it in a 1750 essay—he feared young people (especially young women) would become so addicted to the romantic fantasies of novels that they couldn't adapt to the mediocrity of real life. The worry is almost identical to today's worry about short-drama addiction.

The 19th century produced the penny dreadful: cheap serialized fiction, one penny per installment, one chapter each, ending on a cliffhanger, continuing next week. That's the short-drama structure, isn't it? Pay by installment, hook-based narrative, cheap pricing—people were doing this 170 years ago. Only the medium changed.

Then film, then radio, then television, then the internet, then smartphones, then short video, then vertical short dramas.

Follow this thread and a few trends become very clear.

## Three Curves

The first curve is **the continuous fall of entry barriers**.

Going to church required leaving home, arriving at a fixed place at a fixed time. Reading a novel required literacy. Watching a film required buying a ticket, going out, sitting in a dark hall for two hours. Watching TV required being home, turning it on during broadcast hours. Each time the barrier dropped, the audience grew by an order of magnitude. By the era of smartphones and mobile internet, the barrier fell to nearly zero—all you need is a phone and fragments of time. On the subway, in the bathroom, in the three minutes waiting for delivery, in the half hour before sleep, you can plug into a stretch of substitute reality.

This is not a neutral change. Falling barriers mean the boundary between substitute reality and daily reality is dissolving. In the past, entering and exiting substitute reality was ritualized—walk into the cinema, lights dim, screen brightens, story begins; lights come up, walk out, return to the street. Now that boundary barely exists. You can slide into substitute reality at any moment, get pulled back at any moment, then slide in again. Substitute reality is no longer a place you occasionally visit; it has become a membrane layered over daily life.

The second curve is **the continuous rise of personalization**.

A church sermon was one-to-many broadcast. Novels were publishers guessing what would sell. Films were Hollywood selecting topics through market research and experience. Television added ratings feedback, adjusting content to audience response. But all of these are crude compared to today's algorithmic recommendation.

Short-drama platforms now know when you're most vulnerable (usually late at night), know which story types you respond to most strongly (through your dwell time, completion rate, payment behavior), and know at which narrative node a payment prompt is most likely to make you pay. It doesn't need to "know"—it just feeds millions of users' behavioral data to an optimization algorithm and lets the algorithm find the strategy that maximizes payment conversion. No one there understands you, but the system's behavior looks like it understands you deeply.

The significance of this shift: substitute realities used to be a public good—everyone watched the same film, read the same novel, and you could discuss your shared experience with others. Now substitute reality is increasingly custom-private—what you scroll and what the person next to you scrolls are completely different; you consume your own versions of the dream. This atomization makes collective reflection harder—it's difficult to have a public discussion about something you only experience alone.

The third curve is **the continuous shortening of feedback cycles**.

The satisfaction of a novel usually accumulates over hours or days. A film takes about two hours. A TV episode, 45 minutes. A vertical short-drama episode, one to three minutes. Each shortening trains your brain to accept a shorter delayed-gratification cycle. Once you're used to an emotional climax every two minutes, picking up a novel that needs a hundred pages of setup before it gets going will feel boring.

This is the reshaping of attentional habits. Its effects aren't limited to entertainment consumption—once you're used to the rhythm of short dramas, your attention patterns at work and in study are affected too. This isn't speculation; cognitive psychology research already shows a correlation between sustained short-video consumption and shortened attention spans. The direction of causality is still debated, but the correlation alone is troubling enough.

## A Perverse-Incentive Math Problem

Put these trends together and an uncomfortable economic structure emerges.

A simplified comparison. Suppose you have 10 billion yuan and can invest it in one of two directions.

Direction A: invest in improving reality. Build schools, fix infrastructure, improve labor conditions, narrow the wealth gap. These investments have long payback periods (decades), high uncertainty, low margins, and one fatal feature—if you succeed, you destroy your market. If everyone lives a good life, who still needs to escape reality?

Direction B: invest in substitute reality. Build short-drama platforms, games, social media, virtual worlds. These investments have short payback periods (months), low marginal costs (copying a digital product costs nearly zero), high margins, and one very friendly feature—if reality stays bad, your market persists. Your customers will never be cured, because you sell painkillers, not surgery.

Any rational capital chooses Direction B. No conspiracy required—just the natural logic of the market.

More troubling: a hidden negative feedback runs between the two directions. B's success weakens A's motivation. When people have good enough escape tools, their demand for improving reality declines. Not because they've become stupid, but because improving reality is too hard, too slow, too uncertain, while escape is too easy, too instant, too available. When you can spend 6.9 yuan for a three-minute experience of being a "winner in life," why would you spend thirty years pursuing the same thing in reality?

Guy Debord pushed this logic to a more abstract level in *The Society of the Spectacle* (1967). His core claim: in developed capitalist societies, "spectacle" has become the dominant form of social relation. Debord defines spectacle as "a social relation between people that is mediated by images." In plain terms: your relationship with the world increasingly happens through watching, and less and less through direct participation. You're not experiencing life; you're watching images of life.

When Debord wrote that, the main form of spectacle was television and advertising. If he were alive today and saw a person lying in a rented room "experiencing" power, love, revenge, and justice through vertical short dramas—things he may never touch in reality—he would probably conclude his critique had been too conservative.

## The Triumph of Simulacra

Push the theory one step further. French philosopher Jean Baudrillard proposed an even more radical concept a few years after Debord: the simulacrum.

Baudrillard said the relationship between image and reality passes through four stages. First, the image is a faithful reflection of reality—a photograph records something that really happened. Second, the image begins to mask and distort reality—news photos are cropped and angled to tell a particular story. Third, the image masks the absence of reality—product ads display a lifestyle no one actually lives. Fourth, the image detaches from reality entirely, becoming a "pure simulacrum"—it no longer points to anything real; it is itself the whole.

Short dramas may be sliding toward stage four. Think about it: in a typical comeback drama, the protagonist's "success" corresponds to no possible success path in reality. The ways villains are punished could not happen in the real world. The romantic plots violate every basic rule of how society operates. And none of it stops the audience from enjoying it—because the audience isn't seeking a reference to reality either. They're seeking a pure emotional experience, one that doesn't need to connect to the real world in any way.

This is what Baudrillard called "the desert of the real": when the simulacrum is vivid enough and satisfying enough, nobody turns back to care about the increasingly barren reality. Reality isn't replaced—it becomes irrelevant.

## From Soma to Short Dramas

Pause the theory. Return to a concrete literary image.

Aldous Huxley wrote *Brave New World* in 1932. In that fictional society, the tool rulers use to maintain order isn't the army or prisons—it's a drug called soma. Soma has no side effects and puts you instantly into a blissful trance. Unhappy? Take a soma. Discontent with reality? Take a soma. Want a "vacation" without going anywhere? Take three somas; you can spend a whole weekend inside your head.

One key feature of Huxley's imagined society: nobody feels unfree. Because the loss of freedom took a completely unexpected route—it happened through pleasure. When you can obtain happiness at any moment, why do you need freedom? Freedom is a means to pursue happiness; if the means can be skipped, who cares?

Looking back ninety years later, Huxley's technical details are of course outdated—we don't have that pill. But his intuition about social psychology was eerily accurate. Soma's function—providing an instant, barrier-free, personalized escape hatch when reality turns unpleasant—is now supplied jointly by vertical short dramas, algorithmically recommended short videos, and the entire mobile-internet entertainment ecosystem.

The difference: Huxley's soma was distributed free by the government to maintain social stability; our "soma" is sold by commercial companies for profit. That difference matters. Once the escape tool becomes a business, it has its own growth logic—maintaining stability is nowhere near enough; it must keep expanding the market, increasing user stickiness, and developing new monetization. Its objective function was never "let people just barely endure reality." It is "make people pay and stay as much as possible."

Which means commercialized soma is more corrosive than government-issued soma. The latter has an equilibrium point (social stability achieved, done); the former has none (profit can always be higher).

## The Most Efficient Metaverse

Back to the opening comparison. Mark Zuckerberg spent over $40 billion trying to build the metaverse, and it didn't work well. The short-drama industry achieved larger-scale substitute-reality consumption at extremely low cost. Why?

Because Zuckerberg misunderstood where the bottleneck of substitute reality lies.

He thought the bottleneck was technology—resolution too low, latency too high, headsets too heavy, interaction too unnatural. So he invested heavily in overcoming those technical obstacles. But the fact is: the human brain's requirements for "immersion" are far lower than he thought. You don't need surround sound and spatial tracking to make someone forget their real circumstances inside a story. You just need an emotional hook with enough force.

A crudely made vertical short drama with ordinary image quality—if it pokes one of your emotional sore spots in the first minute (publicly humiliated by a boss, deceived by a partner, looked down on by relatives), you're already "in." Your brain doesn't care about pixel counts and frame rates. It cares about the precision of emotional resonance. A short video costing a few thousand yuan to make can easily beat a VR headset costing a few thousand yuan at this.

From this angle, calling short dramas a "low-spec metaverse" actually underrates them. On the core task of "getting a person into a substitute reality," short dramas are far more efficient than VR. Lower marginal cost, broader audience, more flexible usage scenarios, stronger addictiveness. If we measure by "substitute-reality immersion time produced per yuan invested," short dramas may be the most efficient dream-making machine in human history.

The metaverse's mistake was treating substitute reality as an engineering problem—better hardware, more realistic rendering, lower latency. But it's actually a narrative problem. Humans don't need their eyes fooled. They need their hearts carried away.

## What Comes Next

If we extrapolate along the three curves—falling entry barriers, rising personalization, shortening feedback cycles—one obvious direction is: AI-generated personalized narrative.

Not choosing one drama from a finite library, but AI generating, in real time, a story that belongs only to you, based on your emotional state, personal history, and psychological profile. Just got dumped? It generates a story where your ex regrets it madly. Wronged at work? It generates a story where your boss kneels and apologizes. These stories don't need to be filmed; text is enough—your brain fills in the visuals itself.

This scenario isn't science fiction. As this is being written, people are already using large language models to mass-generate short-drama scripts. From script generation to narrative generation is a single step. When that step is taken, "substitute reality" goes from a mass product to something completely private, completely custom, completely endless.

At that point, the relationship between "reality" and "substitute reality" may undergo a subtle inversion. Today: people hit difficulties in reality and go looking for comfort in substitute reality. Tomorrow: people spend so long in substitute reality that reality becomes an interval to be endured—eating, using the bathroom, commuting, the things you must return to the physical world for, become unwanted interruptions between two immersions.

It sounds absurd. But watch the daily routine of a heavy short-video user, and you'll find this inversion is already partly happening.

## An Ending Without a Conclusion

Writing this, I find it hard to produce a clean conclusion. The topic inherently resists conclusions.

If I say "so we should watch fewer short dramas and face reality more," that's as correct and as useless as telling a heavy smoker "you should quit." The problem isn't individual willpower; it's the structural fact that the addictive thing is easier to obtain than the withdrawal.

If I say "we should regulate the short-drama industry," that's reasonable—but where does the logical boundary of regulation lie? Regulate short dramas and people turn to other forms of escape. Control the app and you still can't control the brain's demand for escape. As long as the root of the demand is untouched—the things that make people's reality unbearable—any supply-side intervention just plugs one hole and opens another.

If I say "the root cause is structural oppression, so we should transform the social structure," that's theoretically correct, but its operational feasibility is close to zero—at least for the individual reading this article.

So all I can really say is this:

Understanding how this system works has value in itself. It may not let you change anything immediately, but it can give you one tiny window of choice the next time you're lying in bed at 1 a.m. about to open a short-drama app—you can open it, or you can not. That window is not a moral judgment. It's not about who is more refined or more base. It's just a window.

Tim Wu, in *The Attention Merchants*, chronicled how human attention went from a public resource to a private commodity, from 19th-century newspapers to 21st-century social media. He ends the book with a plain sentence: the first thing we can do is become aware that our attention is being harvested.

Awareness isn't solution. But without awareness there isn't even the possibility of beginning to solve anything.

That's as far as these three pieces can go. The rest, everyone has to feel out themselves. If you put the phone down after reading and went for a walk, or if you opened a short drama right after reading—there is no hierarchy between those two choices. What matters is that you know what you're doing, and why.
