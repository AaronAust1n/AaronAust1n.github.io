# Achilles' Heel: Old Bottle, New Wine

> You have to give an invincible man a way to die, or nobody watches.

*Published on July 6, 2026*

---

Last week I went to see *Supergirl*. It was bad. Walking out, I told my partner, "So bad, exactly as bad as the internet said, hhhhh—there's this mindset now: it's a bad movie, I went, it was indeed bad, and now I feel settled, hhhhh." But what I was actually thinking about was something else. Not where the film failed (too many places to bother listing)—it was that I spent those two hours with an unnameable boredom, a "I know exactly what you're going to do next" kind of boredom.

On the bike ride home I figured it out. The source of that boredom is ancient. Someone wrote a version of it about three thousand years ago.

![Thetis dipping Achilles into the River Styx, by Donato Creti](/images/posts/achilles-heel-superman/thetis-achilles.jpg)
*Thetis dips Achilles into the Styx. Painting by Donato Creti, public domain.*

Thetis held Achilles by the heel and dipped him into the River Styx. His whole body touched the water—invulnerable, impervious to blade and spear. But she had to hold onto something, and the heel stayed dry. Later Paris put an arrow there, and he died.

I used to find this setup ridiculous. Couldn't she switch hands halfway? Couldn't she lower him on a rope? But then I thought: myth doesn't care about that kind of logic. What it says is: a person cannot be without weakness—not even if your mother is a goddess, not even if you've been dipped in the river of death. **There has to be one place that can die**, or the story can't continue.

This is a narrative problem, not a worldbuilding problem. Make your character invincible and, story-wise, you're finished. No tension, no stakes, the audience can't sit still. So Achilles' heel is, at bottom, a back door the author leaves for himself—somewhere to strike when he finally needs the character dead.

Superman's kryptonite has a far more mercenary origin. Mercenary to the point of comedy.

In 1943, the voice actor for the Superman radio drama needed a vacation. Not a story need—a real person, in the real world, wanted to go on holiday. So the producers came up with something: invent a rock. Superman touches it and collapses, lying there groaning, while the supporting cast bustles around him for a few episodes. When the actor comes back from his vacation, Superman stands up again.

Kryptonite is, at bottom, a scheduling tool. It wasn't a dramatic device designed by some genius writer. It was an HR solution.

![A green fluorite crystal](/images/posts/achilles-heel-superman/green-fluorite.jpg)
*A green fluorite crystal. Real stones, of course, don't paralyze anyone—but the imagery of "a green ore from the homeland" did grow out of minerals like this one. Source: Wikimedia Commons.*

The interesting part comes later. The scheduling patch took six years to reach the comics (1949), and when it did, it was red. Green came two years after that. Even an iconic image like "glowing green rock" wasn't born in one step—it was tried out slowly. And yet it fit, extremely precisely, into the core slot of the Achilles structure: the invincible man must have a place that can die.

Something invented by accident meshed perfectly with a mythic structure three thousand years old. I don't know whether to call that coincidence, or to say the structure itself is simply too strong—so strong that you can throw almost anything into it and it holds.

Still, kryptonite has an advantage over the heel. Where?

The heel is attached to the body. Either the arrow hits it or it doesn't. Narratively, it's a one-time device. You can't shoot him in the heel every week and have him recover every week—that would be absurd.

Kryptonite is an external substance. It can be made into anything: bullets, powder, radiation sources, a gemstone in a ring (Luthor actually did this). It doesn't depend on the hero's body, so it can recur, be used by different people in different ways, and spawn variants—in the sixties, DC produced roughly a dozen colors of kryptonite. Red drove Superman insane, pink made him fall in love, white killed plants.

In other words, the weakness itself was made into an interface. An interface you can keep plugging new modules into. The old bottle can hold new wine indefinitely, because the bottle's mouth was designed as a standard part.

The price, of course, is that overuse makes it worthless. In the seventies, DC published an issue that turned all kryptonite on Earth into scrap, because the writers had truly run it into the ground. Every issue, another rock. The audience went from "Oh god, Superman's going to die" to "Oh, this again." A weakness that shows up too regularly stops being a weakness. It becomes part of the workflow.

Villain finds kryptonite → Superman collapses → friend knocks the rock away → Superman stands up → villain loses. That's not a story. That's a ticket.

*Supergirl*'s problem is on the other side.

It's not that kryptonite is overused—the opposite. It never seems to figure out what Kara's weakness actually is. Watching it, I kept feeling: she could obviously solve this, but the script won't let her, and no reason is given that holds up. It's not "something is limiting her." It's "the screenwriter needs this scene to run five more minutes."

Achilles' heel is at least a rule. This is more like Achilles tripping over his own feet. When it's over, you can't remember what she's afraid of, what actually constitutes a real threat to her.

Which, in reverse, proves the value of that old bottle. Superman has chewed on kryptonite, magic, red-sun radiation, psychological weaknesses for decades—any renovation works, but the bottom line is: there has to be something there. There has to be a rule on the table. Only then can the audience be tense with you, within the rules. When there's nothing, the audience isn't tense. They're just bored. Which is exactly the boredom I felt in that theater.

Writing this, I remember a version that did it better.

*All-Star Superman*, by Grant Morrison. In it, Superman is dying because he absorbed too much solar radiation—the sun is the source of his power, but in excess it becomes a slow poison. The thing that gives you superpowers is killing you.

Why is this setup good? Because the weakness isn't added from outside. It is the power itself. However kryptonite changes its tricks, it remains, at bottom, "there's a rock out there that can beat you"—an externally attached limitation. *All-Star Superman* grows the weakness from the inside: the very mechanism of your strength is the reason you die.

If kryptonite is the industrialized version of Achilles' heel (reusable, extensible, iterable), what Morrison did was return to the older, simpler form—where weakness and strength are one. Thetis dipped her son in the Styx; the river gave him deathlessness. But deathlessness itself means that the fact "one day he will die" becomes extraordinarily heavy. The man you cannot kill—when he finally dies, that blow lands heavier than anyone's.

![The Death of Achilles, by Rubens](/images/posts/achilles-heel-superman/death-of-achilles.jpg)
*The Death of Achilles, by Peter Paul Rubens, public domain.*

![Three forms of weakness: the heel, kryptonite, the power itself](/images/posts/achilles-heel-superman/three-weakness-forms.svg)
*Figure: From the heel in myth, to kryptonite in the comics industry, to writing the power itself as the weakness.*

I'm not sure DC can ever push the Superman system all the way to this. They've brushed against it occasionally, but never held it steadily. Most of the time, they still reach for the rock.

And honestly, reaching for the rock is fine. Audiences have short attention spans. The fact that a three-thousand-year-old bottle still gets used proves the problem it solves is real: you have to give an invincible man a way to die, or nobody watches.

It's only when you can't even be bothered to reach for the rock—like *Supergirl*, letting a character go inexplicably weak at the wrong moment—that you're no longer pouring new wine into an old bottle. You're just holding an empty one.
