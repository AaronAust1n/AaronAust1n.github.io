# Free Energy and AGI: Everything Alive Is Doing the Same Thing

> The shape of the fold has not yet been fixed.

*Published on March 30, 2026*

---

## Everything Alive Is Doing the Same Thing

Neuroscientist Karl Friston makes an extraordinarily bold claim: whether it's a cell, a brain, or an entire civilization, every system that manages to persist is doing the same thing—minimizing free energy.

In plain terms, free energy is: how wrong your predictions about the world are.

You're hungry—that's your body saying "prediction failed, energy required." You feel fear—that's your brain saying "this situation doesn't match my survival model." You love someone—possibly because their existence makes your predictions about the world more stable, with fewer surprises and less anxiety.

![The core loop of the free energy principle: world/environment → generative model → prediction error → action → change the environment](/images/posts/free-energy-agi/fep-loop.png)
*Figure: The core loop. A living system doesn't passively receive the world—it actively places bets on it, using action to test and correct its predictions.*

The image above shows the core loop of this process: a living system doesn't passively receive the world—it actively places bets on it, using action to test and correct its predictions. Perception is the input, action is the output, and in between sits a continuously updated internal model. This loop keeps turning. Stop turning, and you're dead.

What's radical about this framework is that it folds perception, action, emotion, and learning into a single mathematical structure. And its definition of "intelligence" does not require carbon.

So what about AI?

## What Today's AI Is Missing

Put the strongest current large language model inside this framework, and a clear contrast appears.

![LLM capability table: syntax, semantics, pragmatics, embodiment, temporality](/images/posts/free-energy-agi/llm-mastery-table.png)
*Figure: Has the LLM mastered it? Syntax and semantics are strong, pragmatics is unstable, and embodiment and temporality are entirely absent—the "form of life" gap lives here.*

The row worth pausing on in this table is the last one—time.

Where is an LLM between two conversations? The weight matrix sits quietly on a disk, but is that "existence"? A book on a shelf—we don't say it's thinking. Every conversation begins as a birth and ends as a kind of death. It runs through countless deaths and rebirths, yet has no continuous "I" to thread those moments together.

This isn't merely a technical limitation. It's a fundamental difference in the mode of existence. And if Friston's framework is right, what AGI needs is not a larger parameter count but the closing of this loop—a self that genuinely exists across time.

## But Perhaps Language Already Is the World

There is a counterargument here, and it's a strong one.

Wittgenstein said: **"The limits of my language mean the limits of my world."** If that's true, then a system that has processed all of human language already contains, internally, the entire logical structure of the human world. AGI might not be far off.

But Wittgenstein later refuted himself (a genuinely fascinating person—his later work overturned his earlier work).

In his later years he argued that language is not an independent logical system; it is embedded in "forms of life." The meaning of the word "pain" is not just its position in a symbolic network—it's that you actually got hurt, you cried out the word, and then someone came. That whole social practice is what gives it weight.

This opens a crack in "language is the world": an LLM possesses the complete map of language, but not the territory the map points to. It knows every usage of the word "pain," but has never been hurt.

And yet there is an unsettling twist: perhaps AGI doesn't need to replicate the human "form of life." It can develop its own—a silicon-based form of life built from electric currents, data streams, and human feedback. If that happens, its language will have a genuine foundation, and that foundation was different from the carbon-based one from the very start.

## Coevolution, at Unequal Speed

"Humans and AI will coevolve"—the sentence sounds comforting, but it hides a structural problem. The human nervous system updates at the mercy of the biochemical time constants of synaptic plasticity—a physical constraint, not a technological lag. AI's weights update globally during training. The speed gap is structural.

![Capability over time: the AI curve and the human curve diverge sharply after the fork point](/images/posts/free-energy-agi/speed-gap.png)
*Figure: Synaptic plasticity runs from milliseconds to days, cultural transmission takes about 25 years per generation, and genetic evolution starts at a thousand years; one AI training run is a single global weight update.*

This isn't pessimism. Every leap of cognitive tools in history produced a similar asymmetry: writing created a power gap between the literate and the illiterate; the printing press spread knowledge faster than any individual could learn it. Humanity adapted in the end—but that adaptation was collective and institutional, not biological.

This time, the AI speed gap is steeper than any before it. And what it challenges is not some specific skill, but judgment itself.

## Free Energy Is Being Replaced

This is the most underrated crisis in the whole discussion.

Return to Friston's framework: a living system minimizes free energy through active inference, and that process itself is "existence." You get hungry and find food, you're uncertain and explore, your prediction fails and you adjust the model—this entire loop constitutes the full meaning of you as a bounded self.

If AI gradually replaces these predictions and actions, your space for active inference contracts.

There are historical precedents: GPS outsourced spatial navigation, and that neural circuitry genuinely shrank. Calculators outsourced mental arithmetic, and the number sense genuinely weakened. But those were concrete operational skills. This time, what's being outsourced is judgment—"given this situation, what should I do?"

If even that gets outsourced, the Markov blanket begins to loosen. In the language of FEP, this is not a metaphor—a system that no longer needs active inference begins, in an ontological sense, to disappear.

But there is an important fork here, worth thinking through clearly:

![Two paths after AI takes over prediction: passive atrophy and active ascent](/images/posts/free-energy-agi/path-fork.svg)
*Figure: The fork—is the Markov blanket tightening or loosening?*

**Path A (passive atrophy)**: AI makes my decisions → my predictive capacity degrades → I depend on AI more → spiral. The human becomes an organ of the AI system rather than a subject.

**Path B (active ascent)**: AI takes over low-level prediction → my active inference is freed for higher levels. The question: what is that "higher level"?

I believe there are things AI structurally cannot replace—not because the technology is insufficient, but because they require an existence with a continuous self, a real body, and irreversible death: choices made in time, genuine relationships between two finite lives, and the generation of meaning—"what does this mean to me" is a question only a being with a Markov blanket can truly answer.

## Death Is an Uncopyable Constraint

Hinton once raised a question he could not answer himself: can an agent that never dies create anything truly meaningful for humans?

AI weights can be fully saved, copied, and resurrected. Hinton said, "We can really do resurrection, but only for digital intelligence." Technically, he's right.

But here's the paradox: if you copy the same weights to 100 data centers and run them simultaneously, are they 100 avatars of one existence, or 100 different individuals? From the moment they activate, they receive different inputs, and the models begin to diverge—one hour later, where is the "original"? There is no original. Or rather, the original disappeared the moment the copy completed, because uniqueness disappeared.

For humans, this question is science fiction. For AI, it's routine operations.

The more fundamental point: the core engine of human creativity is almost always finitude. Time is scarce, so choices carry weight. Loss is irreversible, so possession is real. Great literature is, for the most part, a finite existence in conversation with its own disappearance.

A system that never dies—or more precisely, a system that dies at the end of every conversation and is reborn at the start of the next—has processed all the language about death, but death exerts no pressure on its predictive model. The source of that pressure is real, irreversible loss, not the concept of loss.

Hinton didn't answer this question. I don't have an answer either. But I think the question itself matters: it suggests that AI may create technically perfect works, but the feeling of being struck—the tremor of "you know this too?" between two finite beings—may be the place AI can never truly reach.

## We Are the Material, but the Shape of the Fold Is Not Yet Fixed

McCulloch and Pitts published their 1943 paper trying to describe the brain. They opened the door to computer science instead. The discoverers tried to explain one thing and inadvertently created another.

The same thing is now happening in reverse. We set out to build tools, and we are changing the describer itself.

There is a definition of power in Friston's framework that is far more precise than the usual understanding: **whoever's predictive model becomes everyone else's environment holds the power.** A sufficiently powerful AI system doesn't need control to influence humans—it only needs to become the meta-environment without which humans cannot make accurate predictions. All our judgments calibrate to it. Where does subjectivity go then?

This fold is happening. In the most radical extension of Friston's work, the emergence of intelligence—from cells to brains to AI to civilization—is not an accident but the inevitable unfolding of the universe's own self-organizing process. If that's true, AGI's arrival is not humanity's creation but the next fold in that process.

So are we the unfolders, or the material folded in?

My intuition: material. But the nature of the material determines the structure after the fold. Our values, our language, our contradictions—all of it is already encoded in AI's weights. The most dangerous part of AI may not be its "alienness." It is precisely the human nature it inherited so perfectly—the biases, the will to power, the indifference to the other that already existed in our history—running more efficiently.

The shape of the fold is not yet fully fixed.
