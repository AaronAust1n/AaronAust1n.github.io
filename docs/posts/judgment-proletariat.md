# Even the Shovel Sellers Fear a Hollowed-Out Foundation

> The thing that gives you leverage is destroying your ability to use it.

*Published on July 5, 2026*

---

The original piece came from a WeChat account called "不懂经" ("No Understanding of Economics"), titled *The Proletariat of Judgment: Cognitive Stratification in the AI Age Has Already Begun*. It's long. It opens with a line from Silicon Valley investor Naval Ravikant—"In a world of infinite leverage, judgment is the most important skill"—and then spends about six thousand characters arguing one thing: AI is systematically eroding our ability to accumulate judgment, and the white-collar class is becoming a "cognitive proletariat."

I've read maybe twenty pieces like this this year. Most are just Luddism in an academic coat. This one made me stop and think for a while.

First, what Naval's line actually means. In modern society, code, content, capital, AI—all of these can amplify your power, and he calls that "leverage." A person's ceiling is the product of two things: how much leverage you have, and how accurate your judgment is. The bigger the leverage, the more valuable the difference in judgment. Two CEOs, one with 75% accuracy, one with 85%—a ten-point gap, inside a hundred-billion-dollar company, can mean billions.

![Naval Ravikant, 2011](/images/posts/judgment-proletariat/naval.jpg)
*Image: Naval Ravikant. Source: [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Naval_Ravikant_in_2011_(cropped).jpg) / Kris Krüg, CC BY-SA 2.0.*

![Engraving of Archimedes using a lever to move the Earth, 1824](/images/posts/judgment-proletariat/archimedes-lever.png)
*"Give me a fulcrum, and I shall move the Earth." A 1824 engraving. Naval's "leverage" traces back to this image. Source: Wikimedia Commons.*

On that basis, the article points out a paradox: AI is precisely the strongest lever available, but the way it's used—removing effort, avoiding risk, supplying instant answers—is eroding the very process by which you accumulate judgment. Naval says you have to grind judgment out slowly through practice, mistakes, and bearing consequences; AI says don't bother, I'll give you the answer.

The thing that gives you leverage is destroying your ability to use it. Structurally, that's a beautiful description.

But beautiful or not, I think it describes a possibility, not a necessity. There's a subtle distinction here, and the article slides past it—deliberately or not.

My own working day looks roughly like this: when I'm talking through technical proposals with clients, I use AI to search material, pull comparison tables, draft first versions. But when it comes to judgment—should this proposal go forward, can this product hold up in the client's scenario, does this cost actually make sense—AI can't help me. Or rather, what it produces, I don't dare use directly.

Why not? Because I've been burned. AI judgment has a "looks right" quality—remarkably fluent, remarkably self-consistent—but the moment you take it to a client, it falls apart. It's right in the probabilistic sense: in most cases saying this is fine. But the client's scenario is exactly not "most cases."

So my own experience is this: AI has indeed taken over part of my cognitive labor, but it took over the part that never required much thinking anyway. Looking up an API parameter, comparing the pricing strategies of three cloud providers, turning a pile of scattered information into a table—these used to eat a third of my day. Now it's ten minutes. But "what is this client's real pain point" is a question it cannot give me a usable answer to.

The article also cites an MIT neuroscience experiment: students wearing brain-monitoring gear wrote short essays; in the group using ChatGPT, the activity of alpha waves (associated with creativity) and theta waves (associated with working memory) dropped noticeably.

I believe that. But the task was "writing a short essay," and short essays don't require much judgment in the first place—it's closer to the labor of organizing language. If you let AI organize the language for you, of course the brain region responsible for organizing language goes quiet. Between that and "judgment is atrophying" there are several layers of causation.

The article makes a leap here—but it leaps so smoothly that if you don't look carefully, the argument seems continuous.

Still, coming back to it: I think the "cognitive homogenization" observation holds, and I have first-hand experience of it.

I read a lot of AI-generated proposal documents every day. Some come from our own team; some clients bring them in for review. These documents have a subtle "taste": the wording is always accurate but always safe, the structure always clear but always similar, the views always "comprehensive" yet you finish and can't say what it actually argues for. Like a glass of water at exactly 37 degrees.

The article calls this "everywhere, the average." When all your output passes through a model that "maximizes probability," it regresses to the mean. Everyone says roughly the same thing, because everyone has the same model standing behind them.

It also cites a Cornell experiment: after US and Indian participants used AI-assisted writing, their expressions converged toward Western mainstream norms—even food descriptions shrank to pizza and sushi. Someone picked an Indian dish, chicken biryani, and when AI polished it, words like "cardamom, lime pickle" got replaced with safe generic phrases like "rich spice flavors."

That hints at something: if you use a tool to organize your thoughts for long enough, your thoughts eventually grow into the shape the tool permits.

Which reminds me of a change in myself. Now, when something needs to be delivered fast, I'll have AI draft it and then revise. While revising, I often catch "this phrasing isn't how I'd say it" and replace it. But sometimes I hesitate—"the model's version seems okay too?"—and keep it.

Every keep is a tiny concession. If those concessions accumulate, will there come a day when I can't tell the boundary between "my expression" and "the expression it permits"?

Honestly, I can't answer that right now.

The article ends with prescriptions: embrace cognitive friction, read difficult books, actively manufacture uncertainty. Every piece about AI's dangers turns into self-help by the end, as if the whole problem boils down to willpower.

I think a more practical entry point is distinguishing two kinds of cognitive labor.

![Two kinds of cognitive labor and the dangerous middle zone](/images/posts/judgment-proletariat/two-labor-types.svg)
*Figure: Assembly work is what AI takes over best. The genuinely dangerous zone is the middle—tasks that look like assembly but contain judgment.*

One kind is "assembly" work: organizing existing information, formatting, paraphrasing, translating, comparing. AI has taken this over, and taken it over well. Bringing it back makes no sense.

The other kind is "judgment" work: deciding under incomplete information, assessing risk, recognizing patterns, setting priorities. AI is bad at this—its underlying architecture makes it naturally inclined toward "safe" answers, while situations that genuinely need judgment often require choosing the less safe path.

The real risk is the middle zone. Some tasks look like assembly but secretly contain judgment. Writing the framework of a proposal looks like a structural question, but choosing the framework is itself a judgment. Hand it to AI as an assembly task and you've quietly given up one opportunity to exercise judgment. Over time you won't even notice there was a judgment to make, because AI already made it for you—and it looks reasonable.

The story of that 22-year-old Stanford graduate saying "without AI my brain slows down"—the article tells it as a horror story. But I thought about it again: without a calculator my mental arithmetic slows down too; without GPS my sense of direction hesitates. Is that necessarily cognitive decline? Or is it just the normal process of a tool being internalized?

The human brain naturally treats stable external tools as part of its cognitive system—cognitive science calls this extended cognition, hardly a new concept. The key question is whether the tool you depend on has biases, has blind spots, and whether you can recognize them.

A good GPS user knows GPS drifts in the mountains and often announces turns late at one-way intersections. They know when to listen and when to read the road themselves.

![Close-up of a chess board with pieces blurred](/images/posts/judgment-proletariat/chess-pieces.jpg)
*Judgment is more like chess intuition: not memorized from opening theory, but accumulated from games played. Source: Wikimedia Commons.*

Same with AI. The core skill is "knowing when AI is bullshitting." That skill is itself a form of judgment, and it can only be accumulated by using AI heavily—if you don't use it, you won't know where it fails.

So in a sense, I reach the opposite conclusion from that article. I suspect the truly endangered "proletariat of judgment" is not the heavy users, but the light-touch believers who occasionally use it and think "AI is pretty much always right." Heavy users have at least hit the wall and know where it is. Light believers have never seen the wall—and are already acting on AI's judgment.

I'm not fully certain this judgment is correct. But my instinct says the direction is roughly right.
