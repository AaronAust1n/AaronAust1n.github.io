# Is Chinese Xianxia Fiction the Low-Spec Metaverse?

*The Low-Spec Metaverse · Part Four*

> In reality, you increasingly learn that so much is not up to you. In the books, the protagonist can push back against everything—through cultivation and luck.

*Published on September 29, 2026*

---

I've been binge-reading xianxia novels lately. Maybe it's a midlife thing—you need something to be obsessed with, something to escape into. Previously it was short dramas. Now it's cultivation.

Moving from short dramas up to novels looks like a downgrade in consumption—it doesn't even need video, just text. But think about it: that's exactly what makes it the lowest-spec, highest-efficiency metaverse. **No headset, no render farm. A text world plus the graphics engine already built into your brain, and you can complete an ascension on the subway commute.**

As I read, I kept finding mapping after mapping between this genre and reality. This piece treats it as field notes: first, why the genre is honest; then, the four or five settings it hides; finally, back to this series' old question—what exactly are we escaping into, and what do we end up reading out of the substitute reality?

## 1 · A Little History: Cultivation Isn't New

Humans have been manufacturing substitute realities for thousands of years.

In 1932, Huanzhulouzhu began serializing *The Legend of the Shu Mountain Swordsmen*, building an entire cosmology of sword immortals, magic treasures, and cave paradises—praised at the time as "a marvel unprecedented in the history of fiction." Go further back: in the 1920s, *The Legend of the Jianghu Knights* was already serializing sword-immortal stories in newspapers. In other words, **the "cultivation universe" was already running as a newspaper serial before television was invented.**

The rest is familiar history: Jin Yong and Gu Long took wuxia to its peak; in the 2000s online literature rose and Qidian industrialized serialization; around 2003, *Piaomiao Journey* turned "cultivation" (xiuzhen) into a standalone genre; after that came *Zhu Xian*, *A Record of a Mortal's Journey to Immortality*, *Battle Through the Heavens*, and on to today's tens of millions of works in progress, plus continuous adaptation into animation, film, games, and short dramas.

From jade slips to newspaper serials, from paid chapters to free infinite scrolling, the medium has changed round after round. One thing hasn't: **the readership is counted in the hundreds of millions.** According to CNNIC, China's online-literature user base has passed 500 million. That means the cultivation world isn't a niche subculture—it's a shared fictional order held by hundreds of millions of people. In *Sapiens* terms, it is a "shared fiction" just like money, nations, and corporations. You don't have to believe qi actually exists; you just need to know, when you read "Golden Core stage," that it sits above "Foundation Building."

And this shared fiction happens to be the cheapest kind: a megabyte of text can carry hundreds of chapters and hundreds of hours of immersion for one person. Measured in "immersion hours produced per yuan invested," it is an order of magnitude more efficient than short dramas—because the rendering cost is outsourced to the reader's imagination.

## 2 · Why Is It Closer to the Truth of Power Than Western Fantasy?

The Western heroic narrative carries a baseline comfort: individual virtue and courage can defeat the system. The cultivation world doesn't say that. It tells you directly: realm determines status, origin determines starting point, resources determine speed. That near-naked honesty makes it the best mirror of reality.

I made a mapping list, and it kept growing:

- **Connections work.** Direct disciples of sect elders, children of great clans—their starting point is someone else's finish line. The trope of "picking up a grandpa" is, at bottom, an external relationship asset.
- **Luck is probability, and also allocation.** Ordinary people need countless strokes of luck to reach a position that may simply be someone else's birth point.
- **Ecological niches get filled.** The balance of power is not a natural state; someone (some character) fills every vacancy. Same in reality—every "opening" gets occupied.
- **Resource scarcity is a hard constraint.** Spirit stones, pills, cave estates—out of reach for ordinary people. Ownership of cultivation resources is a distribution problem, like housing, degrees, and quotas in reality—not an effort problem.
- **Techniques unlock by realm.** This goes deeper than "understanding requires a realm": **your realm determines what you can perceive.** A Nascent Soul cultivator sees what a Golden Core cannot—not because he's smarter, but because his perceptual dimensions differ. In reality, a person in a county town and a person in a core circle literally live in different information universes. Handing them a map doesn't help, because they lack the coordinate system to read it.
- **Above the three layers of opportunity, there is a fourth.** Ordinary people wait for opportunities; the strong participate in them; the top design them—and above the top, there are those who design the rules for "who is qualified to participate in designing opportunities." In cultivation, this is called the Heavenly Dao: not set by any person, but the rules of the rules.
- **Immortality is compound interest, and also fossilization.** An old monster who has cultivated for three thousand years isn't just senior—his vision, planning, and error tolerance belong to a different cognitive species. But many old monsters stop growing after a certain realm and become "living fossils"—which maps to the truest danger of the AI and brain-computer era: the undying are not necessarily the evolved. They may be the most stubborn conservative force.
- **The collapse of the Heavenly Dao maps to ecological overdraft.** Cultivators extract too much from heaven and earth; spiritual energy dries up, a great calamity approaches—the cultivation version of "carrying capacity."
- **War is the chance to get rich, get famous, reshuffle the deck.** One way to reduce resource conflict is to periodically reorder the rankings.
- **Marriage alliances, and the tallest tree catches the wind.** The true top keeps a low profile; alliances are woven from blood and interest.
- **Plots can span centuries.** But to an immortal, "centuries" is still the short term.

The derailments are worth recording too, because they are exactly the genre's **compensatory imagination**:

1. Swift vengeance is the exception; swallowing your anger is the norm—the books reverse it.
2. Offending people is easy to resolve—reality badly underestimates the cost.
3. Harems and wish-fulfillment—packaging resource possession as charm.

## 3 · Four Overlooked Settings That Hide the Genre's Secret

**Spiritual-root inequality is crueler than resource inequality.** The cruelest thing in the cultivation world isn't a shortage of spirit stones—it's spiritual roots: some are born with five roots, trash; some with a single root, genius. It's locked in before the story begins. In reality: genes × early family environment × neurological baseline—all three are almost fully set before you're born. "Effort" is an increment on top of those initial values. And the standard setup of a power fantasy is "born with a trash root, yet blessed with supreme fortune"—the genre's most naked compensatory imagination.

**Inner demons are internalized inequality.** The most dangerous thing in a tribulation isn't the enemy; it's the inner demon—and the worst inner demon is often "believing you don't deserve it." In reality: the most sophisticated thing about systemic inequality is making the underclass internalize its legitimacy as self-assessment—poor because not hardworking enough, powerless because not smart enough. The inner demon isn't external oppression; it's what oppression looks like after it has copied itself into you. This is also the hardest gate on the "path of salvation": you can't even perceive its existence.

**The demonic path is the inevitable product of orthodox monopoly.** The orthodox path is too crowded, resources too scarce—so the system naturally produces a demonic path: faster progress, at the cost of losing control or being hunted. In reality: gray economies, arbitrage, corruption. The demonic path is not individual moral failure; it is the product of misallocated resources. You can't just condemn the demonic cultivators—you have to ask what forced so many into existence.

**Sect vs. rogue cultivator—the undervalued dimension of belonging.** However strong an individual is, a rogue cultivator facing a same-realm opponent backed by a great sect usually loses. The sect provides not just resources but legitimacy, protection networks, and information channels. In reality: which "sect" you come from—company, school, family, city—determines your ceiling more than personal ability. An average graduate of a top school often goes further than a top graduate of a second-tier school—not because they're smarter, but because the sect backing differs. Individual heroism in this dimension is basically an illusion.

## 4 · Why Can't Anyone Write Life After Ascension?

Read enough and you notice a pattern: the hardest part of a cultivation novel to write is what happens after the protagonist becomes an immortal. Many authors' stories collapse right after the immortal tribulation, or descend into infinite power inflation. Because "what comes after" has no answer.

In reality: many successful people reach the finish line they set for themselves and find nothing there—the emptiness after financial freedom, the boredom after reaching the top of power, the void after the peak of fame. **The genre's ultimate bug: its goal system is linear, but the human meaning system needs something nonlinear.** This is the deeper problem behind "the collapse of the Heavenly Dao": when a system's only objective function is to climb, it will eventually hit the day when there is nothing above.

## 5 · The Shape of Salvation: the Tribulation

This series keeps asking about the path of salvation. Interestingly, xianxia offers a more accurate model than most real-world discussions.

In the cultivation world, someone always finds the path of salvation—otherwise the genre wouldn't exist. But that path is never linear accumulation; it is **a discontinuous leap of realm.** A Golden Core cultivator cannot slowly become Nascent Soul by "trying a bit harder": either the tribulation succeeds and the realm is crossed, or you stay at Golden Core forever. The tribulation is a genuinely dangerous moment; many die there.

Map that model onto reality: if a path of salvation exists, its shape is probably nonlinear too—not "a little more effort," but some real systemic leap. And a leap is almost always accompanied by a kind of "tribulation": old cognition, old relationships, old identity breaking down and reassembling at some moment. The difference: the fictional tribulation has clear thunder and a countdown; the real one is silent, and the signal is that you suddenly find you can no longer talk with the people around you.

![Realms as a staircase, breakthroughs as tribulations](/images/posts/xianxia-low-spec-metaverse/realms-and-tribulation.svg)
*Figure: The first three realms are linear accumulation. The tribulation is a nonlinear leap—either you cross it, or you stay where you are.*

This also explains why middle-aged people get obsessed with cultivation novels. It's not just escape—it's unconsciously asking a question: **do I still have a leap left in me?** In youth you believe in accumulation; in midlife you discover accumulation may just be treading water—so you turn toward a kind of "sudden enlightenment" hope: one day, fortune arrives, and the realm changes.

## 6 · Back to the Low-Spec Metaverse

Now the title's question can be answered.

**Yes. Chinese xianxia fiction is the low-spec metaverse.**

It is the cheapest substitute reality ever built: pure text, near-zero marginal cost, endless serialization, privately customized (the recommendation algorithm knows better than you which kind of face-slapping you want). It provides the most complete immersion in the cheapest way, and it outsources the rendering to your own brain. It's even a notch "higher" than short dramas: short dramas dream for you; novels make you participate—you must picture the cave paradises, the sword light, the thunder tribulation yourself.

But it shares the same machinery as short dramas: hooks, dopamine beats, renewals. The difference is that text, cleared of screens, leaves a wider crack for thought than vertical video—sometimes wide enough that you start wondering, "this setting looks like reality."

And that's exactly where its danger lies: **cultivation explains structural problems as personal cultivation problems.** Bad spiritual roots, insufficient resources, a weak sect—these are clearly distribution issues, but the story tells you that as long as fortune comes, the inner demon passes, and the tribulation succeeds, you will leap. This is the power fantasy's strongest anesthetic: it lets you complete your class ascension inside the book, then close it and go back to work.

But seen from the other side, that's also its value: it at least honestly shows the shape of the rules. Most modern narratives won't even show the rules—they only hand you the fairy tale that "effort brings success." Xianxia tells you: the rules are hard, birth matters, the heavens are indifferent—and then it lets you win once, inside the novel.

So my conclusion: it is both a mirror and an anesthetic. The difference isn't whether you read it. It's what you do after—keep tapping to the next chapter, or start asking the more expensive question: **what, exactly, is my realm problem?**
