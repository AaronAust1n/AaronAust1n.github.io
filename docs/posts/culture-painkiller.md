# Cultural Painkillers: When Stories Become Anesthesia

*The Low-Spec Metaverse · Part Two*

> You know short dramas are junk food for the mind. You know the emptiness after. And you keep scrolling.

*Published on June 11, 2026*

---

1:47 a.m. You're lying in bed scrolling a vertical short drama.

You know there's an 8 a.m. meeting. You have decided three times to "sleep after this episode," but every episode ends on the beat that won't let you let go—the heroine is finally about to expose the creep, or the long-bullied side character is about to get his comeback, or the wicked mother-in-law is finally getting slapped to her face. You tap "unlock next episode." A payment page pops up. 6.9 yuan. You pay. Another twist. Another hook. You check the time: 2:23.

![Insomnia at night](/images/posts/culture-painkiller/insomnia.jpg)
*Image: the screen at 2 a.m. Source: [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Depiction_of_a_person_suffering_from_Insomnia_(sleeplessness)_(cropped).png) / myupchar, CC BY-SA 4.0.*

When you put the phone down, you don't feel the fullness of having finished a good book. It's closer to the feeling after a whole bag of chips—there's still flavor in your mouth, but your stomach is empty, and there's a faint nausea. You can't say what those two hours actually "earned" you.

This experience isn't an outlier. In 2023, China's short-drama market reached 37.39 billion yuan. Most of that money comes from small payments—a few yuan per episode, but you pay across dozens of episodes. An average drama runs 60 to 80 episodes at one to three minutes each. The entire viewing experience is designed as a continuous loop of micro-purchases and instant gratification.

Which raises an uncomfortable question: what is this stuff actually doing to our brains?

*Figure: As long as the root of the pain is untouched, the loop keeps turning on its own.*

## Hijacking the Reward Circuit

One thing first: the pleasure you get from a short drama is real. Dopamine really is released; your reward system really is activated. It's not an illusion. The problem is the structure of that pleasure.

Your brain has a remarkably precise reward system. Simplified: you do something good for survival or reproduction (eat food, form social bonds, solve a hard problem), the brain releases dopamine, you feel satisfaction, and that satisfaction makes you repeat the behavior. The system evolved over millions of years and works quite well—in a natural environment.

The trouble is the phrase "natural environment." In nature, reward requires corresponding effort. You get hungry, you find food, chase prey, dig roots—then you eat. Effort and reward have a stable relationship. But when you put a spoonful of refined white sugar in your mouth, the signal strength your reward system receives far exceeds anything it could encounter in nature—and you spent almost no effort. That is a supernormal stimulus: an artificial trigger more intense than any natural equivalent.

What short dramas do to your social-cognition reward system is, in principle, the same thing white sugar does to your taste reward system.

Normally, to feel the satisfaction of "the bad guy is punished," you need a long process in reality—identifying injustice, finding the courage to fight, organizing resources, bearing risk. That process can take months or years. In a short drama, the entire arc compresses into tens of seconds. A slap, a domineering line of dialogue, one twist—the "thrill" lands instantly. Your brain receives the reward signal for "justice has been served." You did nothing.

Psychologist B.F. Skinner studied a reward pattern in the 1950s called variable ratio reinforcement. In plain terms: the timing of the reward is uncertain—you don't know when the next lever press will drop food. Behavior produced under this schedule is the hardest to extinguish—more addictive than fixed-interval reward. Slot machines run on this principle.

The narrative structure of short dramas replicates it precisely. You don't know which episode holds the big twist and which is just setup. But every episode implies "something good is coming next." So you keep tapping, like Skinner's pigeons pecking the button.

## Adorno Said It in 1944

If the critique "mass entertainment is addictive" feels a little trite, your instinct isn't wrong. An important version of this argument appeared in 1944—over eighty years ago.

That year, two intellectuals who had fled Nazi Germany and landed in exile in America—Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer—co-wrote *Dialectic of Enlightenment* in Los Angeles. One chapter is titled "The Culture Industry," and the term became a standard piece of critical vocabulary.

Their argument remains easy to grasp in today's context: Hollywood films, pop music, radio programs—these things look like they offer pleasure and freedom, but their actual social function is to help people endure an unfree life. A worker spends the day in monotonous, repetitive factory work; at night he turns on the radio for a light song, or goes to the cinema for a romantic comedy. The entertainment "restores" him, and the next day he can keep working. It doesn't make him question why his life is the way it is—it helps him forget the question for a while.

Adorno put it sharply: the culture industry offers "fun is the prolongation of work under late capitalism." You think you're resting, but you're actually doing psychological prep for tomorrow's labor. You think you're freely choosing which entertainment to consume, but all the options are industrially produced standards, differing only on the surface—like cars in different colors, all on the same chassis.

Apply that judgment to vertical short dramas and it barely needs editing. You put in overtime in a cubicle all day, or stand on an assembly line for ten hours; at night you lie on the bed in your rented room and open a short drama, watching a protagonist in circumstances just like yours achieve class mobility in three minutes, make the boss kneel, make the cheaters pay. You get a brief pleasure, your mood is repaired to the level of "can still go to work tomorrow." Then you turn off the phone and set the alarm for 6:30 a.m.

Adorno would say: that—no more, no less—is the social function of the short drama.

## But Adorno May Have Been Too Pessimistic

To reduce all mass-entertainment consumption to "anesthetized people receiving industrially produced placebos"—that picture has a cold tidiness, but it may miss something important.

British cultural-studies scholar Stuart Hall proposed a framework in 1973 called Encoding/Decoding. His point: producers of cultural products "encode" particular meanings into them—the director wants you to read something from this shot, the writer wants you to feel something at this plot point. But audiences are not passive containers when they receive it. They "decode" the content through their own life experience, class position, and cultural background, and the meaning they decode is not necessarily the one that was encoded.

Hall distinguished three decoding positions: dominant (fully accepting the author's intent), negotiated (partly accepting, partly modifying), and oppositional (fully inverting the original meaning). Even facing the same short drama, different viewers can come away with entirely different things. One person finishes a comeback story feeling satisfied and no longer needing to pursue anything in reality (the effect Adorno feared); another finishes it suddenly aware of the absurdity of their own situation, angered, and equipped with a language for expressing their discontent.

This distinction matters because it avoids an elitist trap—imagining all consumers of mass culture as brainless masses sitting dumbly on the couch receiving brainwashing. They're not. They have agency. The question is whether agency at the individual level can resist design at the system level.

A person can decode a short drama oppositionally, but the platform's algorithm will not stop pushing. A person can reflect after scrolling that "I just wasted time," but reflection itself doesn't change the product's addiction design. Between individual lucidity and systemic logic there is an asymmetric power relationship.

## Huxley Won

In 1985, American media scholar Neil Postman published *Amusing Ourselves to Death*. It opens with a comparison that has been quoted ever since:

George Orwell, in *1984*, feared that truth would be suppressed by power—governments censoring information, rewriting history, banning speech. Aldous Huxley, in *Brave New World*, feared something else—truth wouldn't need suppression, because nobody would care about truth anymore. People would sink into pleasure, entertainment, and the drug soma, voluntarily giving up the desire to think. Orwell feared external coercion; Huxley feared internal surrender.

When Postman wrote the book, his verdict was: Huxley won. At least in Western democracies, information isn't too scarce but too abundant; people aren't forbidden entertainment but drowned in it; what threatens freedom is not censorship but scattered attention.

Forty years on, Postman's verdict has become more accurate, and in ways more extreme than he anticipated. Postman worried about television; he didn't foresee the one-two punch of smartphones and algorithmic recommendation. In the TV era, you at least had to sit in the living room, at fixed times, choosing from a limited set of channels. In the smartphone era, entertainment is everywhere, anytime, endless—and increasingly precise about what keeps you from stopping.

Huxley's soma in *Brave New World*—a perfect psychotropic drug with no side effects, letting you take a "vacation" from reality at any moment—now looks like a rough prophecy of the vertical short drama. Except soma was a free state ration, while short dramas are paid commercial products. Which makes things slightly worse than Huxley imagined: not only are you being anesthetized, you're paying for your own anesthesia.

## Capitalist Realism

British cultural theorist Mark Fisher published a thin but powerful book in 2009: *Capitalist Realism: Is There No Alternative?* He described a pervasive cultural feeling: people could no longer imagine any alternative to capitalism.

Fisher gave an example: he once taught at a further-education college. His students had a vague awareness of social injustice—they knew something was off—but they couldn't convert that awareness into any form of political action. Their response was to retreat into "depressive hedonia"—a state of perpetually chasing pleasure while never attaining satisfaction. They weren't unhappy; they were unable to be happy, and unable to stop chasing substitutes.

Applied to short-drama consumption, that description has a stinging precision. Much of the core audience—white-collar workers in big cities, factory workers, young people in small towns—are not unaware of their predicament. They know the overtime is too much, the housing prices too high, the channels of mobility too narrow. But between "knowing" and "being able to do something" lies an abyss. Facing that abyss, what short dramas offer is not so much pleasure as **a way of not facing it**.

Fisher quotes Žižek: under contemporary capitalism, the mode of ideology has changed. The old ideology was "they do not know what they are doing" (false consciousness); the current ideology is "they know what they are doing, and they are doing it anyway" (cynical reason). You know short dramas are junk food for the mind; you know the emptiness after; you even know it's a designed addiction mechanism—and you scroll anyway. Knowing the truth doesn't help, because your behavior is embedded in a system stronger than individual will.

## So—Is It "Anesthesia"?

Back to the opening question: are short dramas "anesthetizing the brain"?

If "anesthesia" means some villain in an office plotting—"we'll use short dramas to control the masses' thinking"—then it's a conspiracy theory, unworthy of serious treatment. There is no such plot. Short-drama makers are thinking about how to make money, not how to maintain social order.

But if we understand "anesthesia" as a systemic effect—something that emerges without anyone designing it, simply from market logic running its course—then the word has considerable explanatory power.

The logic runs like this. Capital chases profit. Profit comes from satisfying needs. When reality hurts, people develop a need to escape reality. So capital satisfies that need—by building ever more efficient escape tools. The more efficient the tools, the less people face the real source of the pain. The source is unchanged, so the pain persists. The pain persists, so the need to escape persists. The need persists, so capital remains profitable.

It is a self-maintaining loop. Nobody has to design it. It runs by itself.

One more thing, though. Staying clear-eyed about this structural analysis matters, but so does avoiding a trap: treating everyone who watches short dramas as a passive victim. A person who stood on an assembly line for ten hours and comes home to scroll for an hour—you can say she's being systematically anesthetized, and you can also say she made a rational choice with the resources she has: among extremely limited time, energy, and options, she chose the most accessible form of rest. Both descriptions can be true at once.

Structural critique should point at the structure—at the labor conditions that manufacture this need, at the business model that exploits it, at the policy environment that lets it run unchecked. Not at the individual lying in bed with a phone.

She may already be doing her best.

But how does this loop actually operate? Who profits from it? Where does the money flow? Why is improving reality harder to monetize than manufacturing a replacement for it? Those questions deserve their own piece. Next one.
