# The Flight Simulator in Your Head: Why Humans Can't Live Without Stories

*The Low-Spec Metaverse · Part One*

> Have you ever cried for a fictional person?

*Published on June 10, 2026*

![A campfire at night](/images/posts/brain-flight-simulator/campfire.jpg)
*Image: storytelling around the fire—humanity's first simulator. Source: [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Campfire_flames_at_night.jpg) / Marc-Lautenbacher, CC BY-SA 4.0.*

Have you ever cried for a fictional person?

I don't mean the eyes-get-slightly-moist, quick-wipe, pretend-nothing-happened kind. I mean really cried—nose and tears together, chest tight, unable to recover for a while after closing the book or turning off the screen. For a person who never existed, an event that never happened.

Most people have had this experience. And if you think about it carefully, it's absurd. In that moment your brain knows perfectly well that it's fake—nobody actually believes Anna Karenina lives somewhere in Moscow, nobody believes their friends vanished after Thanos snapped his fingers. You know it's fiction, but your emotional system doesn't care. It grieves when grieving is called for, tenses when tension is called for, rages when rage is called for.

From a pure survival-efficiency standpoint, this system looks wasteful. You sit there for two hours staring at pixels moving on a screen, or at ink on paper, and your heart rate rises, your cortisol fluctuates, your entire emotional system gets dragged around by a fabricated narrative. You could have spent those two hours foraging, socializing—doing anything that actually helped pass on your genes.

Natural selection is stingy about wasted resources. If a trait burns energy without benefit, it usually gets weeded out over the long haul of evolution. But storytelling didn't get weeded out. Every known human culture—without exception—has narrative traditions. From tribes in Papua New Guinea to apartment buildings in Manhattan, humans tell stories, listen to stories, and cry over stories. Which means narrative is either an evolutionarily neutral byproduct (like hiccups—useless but harmless), or an adaptive feature actively preserved by natural selection.

More and more evidence points to the latter.

*Figure: Social trial-and-error in reality is too costly. Stories offer a zero-risk simulator—and they run on the same social-cognition circuitry.*

## A Metaphor About Flying

Cognitive psychologist Keith Oatley proposed a beautiful analogy in his 2011 book *Such Stuff as Dreams*. Fiction, he said, does for social cognition what a flight simulator does for pilots.

The reason flight simulators exist is simple: they let pilots rehearse what you can never safely practice in reality. Engine fires, hydraulic failure, blind landings in a blizzard—if the only way to learn these was through real experience, the tuition would be one airplane and several hundred lives. A simulator lets you run the scenario over and over in a zero-risk environment, so that when the real thing happens, your reactions have already been trained to some degree.

Stories do the same thing for human social life. When you experience a betrayal in a novel, the neural pathways your brain uses to process that betrayal overlap heavily with the ones you'd use if a friend betrayed you in real life. fMRI scans show that when people read a passage about a character feeling sad, the brain regions associated with sadness genuinely light up. Your body's reactions are real—heart rate changes, skin conductance changes, micro-expressions in the muscles—it's just that the input triggering them is fictional.

What's the use of that? The use is that human social life is extremely complex. You need to judge who can be trusted, who is lying, how alliances are shifting, what that sentence the other person just said actually meant. Get these judgments wrong and the price is high—you might lose allies, you might be expelled from the group, and for most of human evolutionary history, expulsion from the group was roughly a death sentence. But you can't learn to avoid expulsion by actually being expelled fifty times. Stories give you a shortcut: mass practice of social reasoning in virtual situations, then carry those lessons back to reality.

Oatley and his colleagues ran a set of experiments with interesting results: people who read a lot of fiction perform better on the "Reading the Mind in the Eyes" test. The test asks you to judge what emotion a person is experiencing from a photograph of their eyes alone. In other words, spending a lot of time inhabiting the mental states of fictional characters—something that looks completely useless—genuinely improves your ability to read people in the real world.

It's the same principle as playing video games to train reaction speed, except what's being trained isn't hand-eye coordination. It's social cognition.

## Why Children Play Make-Believe

If narrative were a learned pastime, you'd expect it to appear in some cultures and be absent in others, like chess or soccer. But if it's a built-in cognitive function, you'd expect it to appear spontaneously in every culture, and very early—too early to have been taught.

And it does: almost every normally developing child begins pretend play at around age two. They treat a banana as a phone, seat stuffed animals for a "meeting," pretend to be firefighters or princesses. Nobody teaches them. No parent sits a child down and says, "Here, let me teach you how to pretend this block is a car." They just start.

Developmental psychologists have observed this for nearly a century. Piaget noticed it in the 1920s and filed it under the "preoperational stage" of cognitive development. But later researchers—Alison Gopnik in particular—argued that Piaget underestimated the complexity of pretend play. Gopnik sees a deep connection between pretend play and scientific thinking: both build hypothetical models and then reason about causality inside those models. When a three-year-old says, "Let's pretend this is a restaurant, you're the chef, I'm the customer, and then a bad guy comes," what she's doing is not fundamentally different, in cognitive structure, from a physicist saying, "Assume the initial conditions are these—what happens inside this model?"

More interesting still: the emergence of pretend play coincides closely with the development of "theory of mind"—the ability to understand that others have beliefs, desires, and intentions different from your own. A child with theory of mind can understand "Mom thinks the chocolate is in the drawer, but I moved it to the cupboard"—she can hold two models of the world in her head simultaneously (the real one and Mom's) and understand that people act on their own models rather than on reality.

This capacity matures around ages four to five, and its first stirrings appear at exactly the age when pretend play begins. Lisa Zunshine, in *Why We Read Fiction*, draws the connection directly: the pleasure of reading fiction comes precisely from the intense exercise it gives our theory of mind—you have to track who knows what, who doesn't know what, who is deceiving whom, who thinks they know but has been kept in the dark. Good mystery novels work because they manufacture masses of this kind of information asymmetry, keeping your theory-of-mind system busy and delighted.

## The Serious Use of Gossip

Anthropologist Robin Dunbar—the man behind "Dunbar's number"—approached the same problem from another direction. He noticed that primates spend enormous amounts of time grooming each other. It looks like hygiene, but it's much more: grooming is the core means by which primates maintain social relationships. Whom you groom, whom you refuse to groom, and for how long—all of it expresses and maintains alliances.

The problem is that grooming has a hard constraint: you can only groom one individual at a time. As group size grows and the number of relationships you need to maintain increases, you must spend more and more time grooming, until you can't do anything else. Dunbar argued that human language evolved, in part, to solve this problem—language is efficient "remote grooming": you can talk to several people at once, and you can maintain a much larger social network by talking about third parties who aren't present.

That is gossip.

Gossip sounds like a dirty word, but Dunbar's research found that roughly 65% of human conversation is social—who did what, who is with whom, who is unreliable, who recently had bad luck. Not because humans are shallow, but because in a species that depends heavily on cooperation, tracking social information is a survival skill. You need to know who makes a good partner, who free-rides, who betrayed whom, and how the power structure of the group is shifting.

Stories, from this angle, are gossip upgraded. They extend the range of social information you can acquire from "the people you know" to "anyone who can be narrated"—including fictional people. Jonathan Gottschall, in *The Storytelling Animal*, jokes that fiction is gossip about people who don't exist. But there's something serious inside the joke: when we gossip about real people and when we track fictional characters, the cognitive systems being used are very likely the same.

## Imagined Communities

If all of the above explains, at the micro level, why individuals need stories, then Yuval Harari's argument in *Sapiens* supplies another piece of the puzzle at the macro level.

Harari's core point is familiar by now: the key to large-scale human cooperation is our ability to believe in "shared fictions." Money is a fiction—the paper itself has no value; it buys bread because buyer and seller both believe in the same story about money. Nations are fictions, corporations are fictions, human rights are fictions, laws are fictions. None of these have counterparts in the physical world; they exist only in humanity's collective imagination. But it's precisely because of these shared fictions that strangers can cooperate—you're willing to hand over the fruits of your labor to someone you've never met in exchange for a few pieces of numbered paper, because you both live inside the same story called "the monetary system."

From this angle, narrative capacity is not entertainment or luxury at all. It is the underlying infrastructure of human civilization. Without the ability to tell and believe stories, there would be no money, no law, no religion, no nations—and no stable cooperative group larger than 150 people. The reason we diverged from chimpanzees is, to a large extent, that we can create, spread, and collectively believe in fictional narratives.

This pushes the question "why do humans need stories" to a deeper level. Stories are not just an individual psychological tool (though they are that too); they are also the glue of social organization. A group coheres because its members share a story about themselves—who we are, where we came from, what matters to us. National narratives, religious narratives, family narratives—all of them do this work.

## The Hero's Journey, and Narrative Standard Parts

Joseph Campbell published *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* in 1949, proposing a bold claim: the world's myths all follow the same structural template, which he called the "Hero's Journey"—the hero hears a call, leaves the ordinary world, undergoes trials, gains some power or wisdom, returns to the ordinary world bearing a gift.

Campbell's specific theory is heavily contested in academia; many anthropologists argue he oversimplified differences between cultures, forcing wildly different narratives into one template. The criticism is fair. But even granting that Campbell's framework is too coarse, one hard-to-ignore fact remains: certain narrative structures do recur across cultures. Predicament, struggle, transformation—these three elements appear in nearly every known narrative tradition.

Why? A plausible explanation is that these structures correspond to universal human survival challenges. Everyone faces difficulty, uncertainty, and moments that demand change. Narrative offers a way to rehearse such situations—in a story, you pre-experience failure and success, you see the consequences of different choices, you process intense emotion at a safe distance. When you meet a similar situation in reality, your brain already has some reference point.

It's like why almost every culture developed some form of cooking—not because people copied each other, but because applying heat to food solves a universal problem (better digestion and safety). The universal problem narrative solves is: how to make decisions in a social environment full of uncertainty.

## From Cognitive Need to Escapism

After all this, someone might ask: are you whitewashing short dramas and junk fiction? Humans need stories, so binge-watching vertical short dramas for three hours counts as "cognitive training"?

No. The distinction matters.

Narrative capacity is neutral infrastructure, the way appetite is a neutral physiological function. Appetite kept you alive in environments where food was scarce—that's adaptive. But when the food industry crafts "supernormal stimuli" with precisely calibrated sugar, salt, and fat, your appetite gets hijacked—you eat far more than you need, because your satiety-signaling system didn't evolve to handle that intensity of stimulation.

The same thing is happening in narrative. Your brain evolved a hunger for stories; that's normal. But when short dramas compress narrative to its extreme—a twist every minute, an emotional climax every three minutes, every second engineered to maximize your emotional response—it becomes the ultra-processed food of narrative. Facing that concentration of stimulus, your cognitive system behaves like your appetite system facing a bucket of fried chicken: it loses control.

Humans need stories the way humans need food. But between "need" and "addiction" lies a very important line. Where that line is, how it gets drawn, and who erases it—that's the subject of the next piece.
